Dynamic Negative Sampling (DNS), introduced by Zhang et al. (SIGIR 2013) as a follow-up to Rendle's BPR, replaces uniform-random negative sampling with a two-step scheme: for each positive (user, item) pair, draw a small pool of H≥1 candidate items uniformly from the user's uninteracted items, score them all with the current model, and pick the highest-scoring one as the "hard" negative for that gradient step. This directly targets the informative region near the decision boundary (items the model currently ranks too high), which the paper shows yields significant AUC/top-N gains over uniform BPR sampling while also speeding convergence, since gradients from easy (already correctly-ranked) negatives are mostly wasted. Later theoretical work (arXiv 2302.03472, "On the Theories Behind Hard Negative Sampling for Recommendation") shows BPR+DNS acts as an exact one-way partial-AUC estimator, formalizing why it helps, and arXiv 2403.19276 discusses guarding against false negatives when H grows large — relevant caution for a popularity-skewed catalog like KuaiRand-Pure where popular unclicked videos can be false negatives rather than true dislikes. The technique is lightweight: it needs only re-scoring a small per-positive candidate pool (e.g. H=5–20) each training step/epoch via a forward pass and an argmax, so it is straightforward to implement with plain numpy (matrix-vector dot products for scores, np.argmax over the pool) with no external library, at the cost of H× more score computations per positive sample than vanilla BPR.
